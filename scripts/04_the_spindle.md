# The Spindle: Gjaus, the Star-Tree

**A deep-lore script for Caves of Qud.** Episode 4 in the series.
Target runtime: 22-25 min · target wordcount: ~3600 words.

Continuity: assumes Episodes 1-3 (Resheph, Barathrum, Rebekah). The
Seraph, the Coven, the thousand-year accord are recapped only where
they bear on the Spindle as an object.

**Strict-evidence rule** (per `feedback_qud_script_strict_evidence`):
every lore claim in this script comes from the extracted corpus or
the decompiled code. Every quote is sourced. Inference is labeled.
The Caves of Qud wiki and YouTube footage are used ONLY for the
visual-asset suggestions at the end — never as a lore source for the
narration.

---

## [COLD OPEN — 0:00]

`[VISUAL: the Spindle on the horizon, seen from a great distance —
a chrome line that runs from the ground past the top of the sky.]`

You can see it from almost anywhere. In the flower fields of Bey
Lah, the hindren glimpse it: a book of love set there tells of a
moment *"at twilight in the shadow of the spindle."* *(book: Love
in Bey Lah)* A poet writes, simply, *"and so my heart ascends the
spindle's height."* *(book: Sonnet)* An aphorism about birds places
it at the edge of the world: *"Atop the Spindle, where debtors and
birds go."* *(book: Aphorisms about Birds)*

It is the tallest thing in the world. It runs from the banana
groves of the north up past the vault of heaven. Every culture in
Qud has a name for it and a story about it, and almost none of
them know what it actually is.

This is the Spindle. And this episode is about the single object
around which the entire late game of Caves of Qud — and, it turns
out, the entire secret history of the world — is wound.

`[TITLE CARD: THE SPINDLE: GJAUS, THE STAR-TREE.]`

---

## [I. WHAT YOU SEE AT OMONPORCH — 1:30]

`[VISUAL: the base of the Spindle at Omonporch; banana (musa)
groves; the self-styled court of Asphodel.]`

The base of the Spindle sits at a place called Omonporch, in the
banana groves — *musa*, the game calls the fruit. When you arrive,
you are greeted by the self-proclaimed lord of the place:

> *"Welcome to Omonporch, traveler. Enjoy your visit to my*
> *earldom. Feast your eyes upon my tall and glorious Spindle.*
> *Take in the scent of roasted musa."*  
> — *(conversation: Asphodel)*

This is Asphodel, Earl of Omonporch. Xe is an Earl by xer own
decree and nothing else. Ask whether xe is truly the Earl, and
the answer is gloriously circular:

> *"Indubitably, for I've proclaimed it so! There was no Earl to*
> *tell me otherwise."*  
> — *(conversation: Asphodel)*

The Barathrumites — Barathrum's guild, who need the Spindle for
their ascent — must *lease* it from this self-appointed aristocrat.
Asphodel demands a price: convene a council of allied factions, the
First Council of Omonporch, and appease xem. (If you happen to be
*Loved* by the Consortium of Phyta, xe folds instantly: *"Oh...
oh! It's you! You are a dear friend, indeed."*) When the deal is
struck, Asphodel appoints *you* xer viceroy over the Spindlegrounds,
welcoming you *"to court, asphodelyte. You serve at my pleasure."*

The quest that formalizes this is called *The Earl of Omonporch*,
and its own gospel-text is wry about what you've done: it records
that *"=name= appointed the corrupt administrator Asphodel as earl
and minister of Omonporch."* *(quest: The Earl of Omonporch)* The
hagiograph names xer in full: *"Asphodel the Lovely, earl of
Omonporch and Protector of the Spindle Gjaus."*

Note that word — *Gjaus.* It's about to matter.

---

## [II. THE NAMES — 4:00]

`[VISUAL: slow push toward the Spindle; overlay the three names as
they're spoken.]`

The most important single conversation about what the Spindle *is*
comes from an unexpected source: a talking tree named Haddas, the
mayor of the village of Ezra at the Spindle's base. Ask Haddas
about the structure and he gives you its names across the ages:

> *"Aye, the Blue Mother who breaks the sky. Those who Eat called*
> *her Gjaus, and those who Wake call her Spindle."*  
> — *(conversation: Haddas)*

Three names, three eras. *The Blue Mother who breaks the sky* — the
poetic descriptor. *Gjaus* — the name used by *"those who Eat,"*
the Eaters, the precursor civilization. *The Spindle* — the name
used by *"those who Wake,"* the people of the present age, the ones
living in the long aftermath.

Haddas also calls it, elsewhere in the same conversation, the
*Star-Tree*, and describes Ezra as the place where the Eaters
*"buried their dead under its roots."* *(conversation: Haddas)* A
tree whose roots hold the Eater dead and whose crown breaks the
sky. (We covered Ezra and that burial ground in the previous
episode, on Rebekah.)

So before we even ask what the Spindle does, the game has told us
what it *is* to the people who live around it: a mother, a tree, a
thing that breaks the sky — and, to the civilization that built
it, a thing with a proper name. Gjaus.

---

## [III. WHAT IT IS — 6:30]

`[VISUAL: a diagram or evocative shot — something conveying
"elevator to the heavens."]`

The plain mechanical answer comes from Barathrum the Old. When the
signal began broadcasting from the Spindle's top, it confirmed his
long-held hypothesis:

> *"The beacon confirmed my hypothesis. The Spindle is an elevator,*
> *engineered by the Eaters to convey freight to and from the vault*
> *of heaven."*  
> — *(conversation: Barathrum)*

An elevator. Freight. To and from the vault of heaven. Barathrum
continues with the history we've traced across this whole series:

> *"in the earliest aurora of our past, the Eaters of Earth were*
> *joined by a great coven of beings that spanned the firmament.*
> *But the Eaters succumbed to some terrible temptation, and an*
> *injunction was placed on our world. Since then, the stars were*
> *silent to us, and our world was left to molder and decay."*  
> — *(conversation: Barathrum)*

So the Spindle is the *infrastructure of a relationship that
ended.* It was built by the Eaters to move freight between Earth
and the Coven of star-spanning beings. When the Coven placed its
injunction and departed, the Spindle remained — a dead elevator to
a heaven that stopped answering. Until, in Barathrum's late years,
the signal resumed: *"an entity roosts atop the Spindle, and
invites us to join it."*

Not everyone has Barathrum's certainty. A contemplative named
Hortensa floats a stranger theory — *"a pet theory of mine that
the Spindle is a liquid in laminar flow."* *(conversation:
Hortensa)* The corpus presents this as exactly what Hortensa calls
it: a pet theory. The game lets its scholars disagree.

---

## [IV. WHAT IT MEANS — 9:30]

`[VISUAL: montage — a Templar outpost, a grieving father in a musa
grove, a bird at altitude.]`

Whatever the Spindle *is* mechanically, what it *means* to the
cultures of Qud is its own subject — and the books are where this
lives.

To the Putus Templar, the order of zealots, the Spindle is a
metaphor for purity of lineage. Their values, one book records, are
*"dedication to a family tree as tall and unbranching as Qud's
Spindle, complete subjugation of those they consider lesser."*
*(book: Murmur's Prayer)* The tallest, straightest thing in the
world becomes the image of a bloodline that admits no branches.

And then there is the most beautiful single text in the corpus that
touches the Spindle — a short story the game files under the title
*The Girl in the Sky.* A father named Aphir lives in the musa grove
at the Spindle's base with his daughter, a child who *"never
fixated her hyaline eyes upon the limbed figures and faces tending
the grove before her, but would instead cast them into the black
yonder beyond the Spindle to gleam in wonder at a burning star."*
*(book: The Girl in the Sky / TeleporterOrbs)* One night —
*"the first night of the Ut yara Ux"* — she does not come home.
Aphir searches the villages and finds nothing. He grieves for
seasons. And the story ends:

> *"on sacred nights he would gaze over the Spindle, mantled in a*
> *thousand lights, and upon the field of burning stars. And he*
> *knew his jewel was among them, for she belonged not of his*
> *world, but of theirs."*  
> — *(book: The Girl in the Sky)*

The script will not over-explain this story. The game places it,
deliberately, at the foot of an elevator to the stars — a structure
that the Eaters built precisely to *convey freight to and from the
vault of heaven* — and lets the reader sit with what it might mean
that a child her father came to believe *"belonged not of his
world, but of theirs"* disappeared from a grove in the Spindle's
shadow. That juxtaposition is in the corpus. What it adds up to,
the game leaves to you.

## [V. THE BARRIER — 13:00]

`[VISUAL: the Spindle pulsing with an interfering field; a climber
design schematic.]`

Here is the obstacle that drives the entire endgame. You cannot
simply climb the Spindle. It defends itself. Barathrum explains:

> *"The climber design relies on the use of electromagnets to*
> *interface with the planet's magnetic field and generate torque.*
> *Unfortunately, the Spindle generates its own interfering field,*
> *perhaps as a mechanism to prevent unwanted ascension."*  
> — *(conversation: Barathrum)*

A mechanism to prevent unwanted ascension. The Spindle is locked.
And the lock can only be released from inside:

> *"the field can be turned off from a place called Brightsheol,*
> *located in the Thin World and accessible only through Resheph's*
> *tomb inside the Tomb of the Eaters."*  
> — *(conversation: Barathrum)*

This is the hinge of the whole structure. To climb the elevator
the Eaters built, you must first descend into the Eaters' tomb,
find the burial chamber of the Seraph who locked it, and pass
beyond into a place called Brightsheol to throw the switch. The
way up runs through the way down.

---

## [VI. THE ASCENT — 15:30]

`[VISUAL: the quest path — the Death Gate, the Tomb interior, the
golem creature, the climb.]`

The mechanical spine of the ascent, from the quest text, has six
steps *(quest: Tomb of the Eaters)*:

1. **Recover the Mark of Death** — *"the lost sigil used by the
   Eaters to mark their dead."*
2. **Inscribe the Mark** — *"Tattoo the mark on your body."*
3. **Enter the Tomb of the Eaters** — through the sealed Death Gate.
4. **Ascend the Tomb and Cross into Brightsheol** — *"find Resheph's
   burial chamber. From there, discover a way to travel to
   Brightsheol."*
5. **Disable the Spindle's Magnetic Field** — *"Access the Spindle's
   control unit and turn off the field."*
6. **Return to Grit Gate.**

Barathrum gives you the reasoning in his own voice. The Tomb is
sealed — *"Resheph sealed the gates a thousand years ago, but
there's a flaw in the seal."* The Mark of Death, *"lost to time,"*
will open the Death Gate *"as it did for countless Eater cadavers
in the long-blurred past."* *(conversation: Barathrum)* You become,
in effect, a corpse the Tomb will admit.

And to make the climb itself, the Barathrumites can't build a
conventional machine in time — the Putus Templar are closing in. So
they build something else. The plan, in the words of the operating
manual written by the mushroom-engineer Pax Klanq and *"edited for
clarity by Q Girl"*:

> *"Klanq and prickles make bioelectrical wonder to form large*
> *creature! You operate. Creature for Spindle climb, but also*
> *walks about and crush things."*  
> — *(book: Operating Manual for Large Creature)*

A living creature, grown from bioelectrical materials, that you
pilot from a control pit inside its body. When it's ready, the
manual says: *"travel to Spindle at Omonporch, enter Spindle
channel, engage Spindle lock-on, and climb!"*

---

## [VII. BRIGHTSHEOL AND THE SHOMER — 18:30]

`[VISUAL: the threshold of Brightsheol; a robed, faceless figure
speaking in archaic script.]`

Cross beyond Resheph's burial chamber and you reach the high gate to
Brightsheol, kept by a figure called Rainwater Shomer. *Shomer* —
the word recurs as *shomrim* in the plural — speaks in the same
deliberately archaic Middle English the game reserves for its
oldest voices (the same register as the Song of the Sky-Bear). The
figure greets you with a ritual:

> *"Saad, welcom. Saad, from thyn heres shaken the wet and olde lif.*
> *Saad, in-to a smal vessel pouren hit. Saad, to thyn sash attachen*
> *the vessel. Saad, crossen nou thrugh the heigh gate and in-to*
> *Brightsheol."*  
> — *(conversation: Shomer)*

It calls you *Saad* — the same title the Song of the Sky-Bear gives
Saad Amus ("highte Amus-an a sturdy Saad"). The player can even try
to claim the title for Resheph at this gate — *"Saad Resheph stands
before you"* — and the Shomer flatly denies it: *"No Saad Resheph
residen her."* Ask whether you are dead, and the answer is:
*"Saad, the bodi dien, but the mind romen"* — the body dies, but
the mind roams. Ask what Brightsheol
is, and the Shomer gives the line that reframes the whole climb:

> *"Alofte of Gjaus a cite twinklen. And ther the Seraph dremen.*
> *And Brightsheol is the drem."*  
> — *(conversation: Shomer)*

Aloft of Gjaus — the Spindle — a city twinkles. And there the
Seraph dreams. And Brightsheol *is the dream.* Brightsheol is not
merely a place atop the Spindle. It is the Seraph's dream of a city,
and you are crossing into it. The shomrim are its keepers, and they
will only disable the magnetic field when the Seraph assents: *"the
Seraph sprecen. Saad, thu asken, and the shomrim assenten."*

The Shomer is not the only way the corpus shows toward Brightsheol.
There is a second, separate mechanism: entombment by Herododicus —
the same Imperial Biographer from Episode 1, the one still waiting
to *"canonize"* the Coiled Lamb's deeds. He explains the
requirement in his own deluded way: *"You must be dead. Dead and
entombed. The body remains, but the mind wanders..."* When the
player asks to be entombed in order to *"travel to Brightsheol,"*
he describes his process — *"First, I image your head, read your
self's narrative from the folds of your brain... Then I...
stylize... the story, and I engrave it on the walls. Once I'm
done... then you can entomb [yourself]."* *(conversation:
ImperialBiographer)* The corpus presents the Shomer's high gate and
Herododicus's sarcophagus as two distinct routes into the Seraph's
dream; it does not chain them into one sequence, and neither will
this script. What they share is the same precondition the whole
Spindle enforces: to go up, you must first be, in some sense, dead.

---

## [VIII. WHAT'S AT THE TOP — 21:00]

`[VISUAL: the apex; the signal; the Seraph; a glimpse of a starship.]`

What waits at the top has been the spine of this entire series. The
signal, Barathrum says, *"is a beacon of welcoming, and it
originates from the top of the Spindle."* *(conversation: Barathrum)*
At the apex *"roosted a Seraph, one of the great machinic archons of
the Eaters"* *(hidden conversation: Barathrum)* — the being who
became Resheph, whose story Episode 1 told.

And there is one more thing up there, which Barathrum reveals only
in his late, unstrung state:

> *"I know... there is a functioning starship atop the Spindle. We*
> *can take it, =name=! We launch ourselves into the deepness of the*
> *dusted cosmos and find the starshiib!"*  
> — *(hidden conversation: Barathrum)*

An elevator to the vault of heaven, built by a civilization that
once traded freight with the stars — and at the top of it, still,
a functioning starship. The Spindle was never only a tower. It was
a *departure point.* The girl in the sky, the debtors and birds,
Barathrum's starshiib dream, the Seraph who came down it and never
left — every story about the Spindle is, underneath, a story about
leaving the world by it.

---

## [IX. THE QUESTION — 23:30]

`[VISUAL: the Spindle at dusk; pull back until it's a thin line
against the stars; hold.]`

In a workshop in Qud, a contemplative named Iseppa sorts screws and
fasteners and thinks aloud. Among the things she is contemplating:

> *"I am contemplating the Spindle's place in Qud, its position as*
> *a conceptual focal point. How much of what we know of the*
> *Spindle is the craft of those before us, carried forward?"*  
> *"I am considering narrative inheritances. The stories we were*
> *told when our minds were more putty-like, the follies that*
> *shaped who we are, that shape the whole of Qud."*  
> — *(conversation: Iseppa)*

That is the question the Spindle finally asks. It is the tallest
thing in the world, and almost nobody who lives beneath it knows
what it is. The Templar made it a symbol of unbranching bloodline.
A grieving father made it the doorway his daughter left through.
The Mechanimists revere the being at its top. Barathrum spent a
thousand years trying to climb it and, at the end, just wanted to
fly away on the ship parked at its peak. Every one of them inherited
a Spindle built by someone else, for a purpose that ended before
their histories began, and made it mean what they needed it to mean.

*How much of what we know of the Spindle is the craft of those
before us, carried forward?* The game does not answer Iseppa's
question. It just leaves the Spindle standing there — Gjaus, the
Star-Tree, the Blue Mother who breaks the sky — running from a
burial ground full of the old dead up past a vault of heaven that,
for ten thousand years, went quiet.

In the next video, we'll go all the way up — into Brightsheol, the
Seraph's dreamed city, and the choice the game finally hands the
player at the top of the world.

`[END CARD.]`
