# Rebekah and the Daughters of Exile

**A deep-lore script for Caves of Qud.** Episode 3 in the series.
Target runtime: 20-22 min · target wordcount: ~3300 words.

Continuity: this episode assumes the viewer has seen Episodes 1
(Resheph) and 2 (Barathrum). The relevant background — the Coven,
the Seraph atop the Spindle, the plagues, the thousand-year accord
— is recapped briefly where it bears on Rebekah's story. The
episode's center is what *she* did and what her acolytes inherited.

**Strict-evidence rule** (per `feedback_qud_script_strict_evidence`):
nothing in this script unless it comes directly from investigation
of the extracted corpus. Every claim sourced. Every inference
labeled. Where the source declines to specify, the script declines
to specify.

---

## [COLD OPEN — 0:00]

`[VISUAL: a quiet headstone in the ruined village of Ezra. Moss
on the carving. A man kneels nearby. A woman watches from a
distance.]`

There is a grave at Ezra. Of all the places to bury someone in
Caves of Qud, this is the most pointed one. The villagers there
call her grave by no special name. The Daughters of Exile call
it sacred. The man named Zothom the Penitent kneels at it most
days, hoping that proximity will give him a credo. The woman
named Yla Haj watches him, and finds him a fool.

The grave belongs to a physician. A teacher. A master machinist.
A healer. The founder of an order called the Daughters of Exile.
The Daughters call her *Rebekah the Exile.* By the public record
of Qud — the version a curious traveler will hear from the
villagers nearby — she was *"a physician, and an advisor to
Resheph until she lost his favor. When, after years of exile,
she lost her voice and her life, she was buried here, an
outsider unfit to be interred with the honored dead."*
*(conversation: Zothom)*

That is the official account. It is not wrong. It is also not the
whole story. The whole story is what ended her life — and what
the quiet around her grave is still holding.

`[TITLE CARD: REBEKAH AND THE DAUGHTERS OF EXILE.]`

## [I. WHAT THE WORLD WAS TOLD — 2:00]

`[VISUAL: a slow tracking shot of the Ezra village, the headstones,
the bas-reliefs Haddas mentions.]`

Two villagers at Ezra will speak to a traveler about Rebekah. They
emphasize different things.

Yla Haj, a tinker-nun who lives by the gravesite, gives one
version:

> *"Do you know the story told of Rebekah the Exile? She was a*
> *teacher to Resheph, but he excommunicated her from the*
> *sultanate and banished her from the realm."*  
> — *(conversation: Yla Haj)*

A teacher. Excommunicated. Banished. The standard shape of an
advisor's fall.

Zothom the Penitent, who watches the grave from camp, gives a
fuller version, framed in the language of medicine:

> *"Rebekah was a physician, and an advisor to Resheph until she*
> *lost his favor. When, after years of exile, she lost her*
> *voice and her life, she was buried here, an outsider unfit to*
> *be interred with the honored dead. So she rests here."*  
> — *(conversation: Zothom)*

A physician. An advisor. Lost his favor. Years of exile. Lost
her voice. Lost her life. Buried as an outsider. None of this
is contradicted by anything else in the game. It is just
incomplete.

What is missing from both accounts — what neither speaker quite
knows — is *what she lost his favor over.*

For that, you have to climb the Spindle. And you have to ask
Barathrum.

---

## [II. WHAT BARATHRUM CALLED HER — 4:00]

`[VISUAL: Barathrum's candle-dim study under Grit Gate. The same
imagery as Episode 2.]`

In the late hidden conversation under Grit Gate — the conversation
the game's own data file flags `ExcludeFromCorpusGeneration='true'`
— Barathrum the Old gives a different account of the same person.
Not contradictory, just deeper.

> *"Through my work I caught the attention of a master machinist,*
> *unconventional of method, who took me under her wing and, in*
> *time, availed me of the orphic truth."*  
> — *(hidden conversation: Barathrum)*

The master machinist's name was Rebekah. So she was *also* a
tinker. A machinist. Someone Barathrum, the eldest and wisest
tinker now alive in Qud, called *his* teacher.

And later, when asked why Rebekah was a healer but the order she
founded is full of tinkers, Barathrum offers what might be the
single most beautiful line in the corpus:

> *"She was a healer, yes. The wire cutter can perform the work*
> *of a scalpel; and the soldering iron, a suture. To take apart*
> *a history, and stitch something new..."*  
> — *(hidden conversation: Barathrum)*

Pull the descriptors together. Yla Haj: *teacher.* Zothom:
*physician, advisor.* Barathrum: *master machinist, healer.* She
was, in different witnesses' frames, all of these. The thread
that ties them is the verb in Barathrum's final clause — *to
take apart a history, and stitch something new.* Whatever
Rebekah did, her tool was the boundary between the medical and
the mechanical, and her object — in Barathrum's exact phrasing —
was *a history.*

## [III. WHAT SHE SERVED — 6:30]

`[VISUAL: the Spindle on the western horizon, before the era of
the Sultanate.]`

This part of the story requires the viewer to remember the central
revelation of the first two episodes. Atop the Spindle has lived,
for an unknown span of time, a Seraph — *"one of the great*
*machinic archons of the Eaters."* When the Coven of star-spanning
beings placed an injunction on Earth and departed, the Seraph
remained behind.

According to Barathrum, Rebekah served that Seraph:

> *"Atop the implausible rise of the Spindle roosted a Seraph,*
> *one of the great machinic archons of the Eaters, and this*
> *being Rebekah served."*  
> — *(hidden conversation: Barathrum)*

This is the load-bearing detail. Rebekah was not, primarily, a
village physician. She served a cosmic intelligence at the top
of the world's tallest tower. She was its representative on the
ground. She knew its plans.

And then, in the brief years before Barathrum's refugee clan
arrived in Qud, the Seraph decided that *"all higher life on the*
*planet must... be purged."* The Coven was returning. The world,
in its current form, was deemed unworthy of receiving them. The
solution was a Gyre of engineered plagues that would kill
sapient life while preserving the architecture of the previous
age.

Rebekah, who served the Seraph, knew this was the plan.

She had a choice.

## [IV. THE BETRAYAL — 9:30]

`[VISUAL: Rebekah in her workshop. Barathrum, a young bear,
listening.]`

Here is Barathrum's exact phrasing of what Rebekah did:

> *"Rebekah, dear tutor, betrayed Resheph's confidences and*
> *apprised me of the plan, so that I might help her dash it*
> *apart and save the peoples of our world."*  
> — *(hidden conversation: Barathrum)*

She betrayed her cosmic master. She told her other apprentice —
a young urshiib refugee newly arrived from the Homs Delta — what
the Seraph intended. She chose to bring him in. She asked him to
help her stop it. The source does not narrate his answer, only
its outcome: the two of them went up the Spindle together.

> *"Together we beseeched Resheph, begged them to reverse the*
> *Gyre."*

They did not, in the end, achieve abolition. What they achieved
was a thousand-year stay of execution. Barathrum would administer
the *earthly plan* — dismantling the Sultanate, reforming Qud
into something the Coven might find acceptable. Resheph would
continue preparations at the *astronomical tier*. And, as Episode
2 documented, the Seraph "insisted on reformatting their
personality as a triumvirate, seeded from thin scans of the
original archon, Rebekah, and myself."

A thin scan of Rebekah's mind became part of the personality that
would later be revered as Sultan Resheph — the figure the
Mechanimist faction worships, carrying a +50 worship attitude
toward him in the game's faction data *(faction: Mechanimists)*.
The game text does not specify which of the three public personae —
the Healer, the Coiled Lamb, the Above — Rebekah's scan ended up
animating. The inference toward *the Healer*, given that she was
*the physician*, is tempting. The source declines to confirm it.

What the source *does* tell you is that, after all of this, Rebekah
did not get to live out her thousand-year project in peace.

## [V. THE ROTTING TONGUE — 12:30]

`[VISUAL: a long shot. A lone figure walking away from a city.
Camera holds.]`

The accord held. Then, at some point, it broke for *her.* Barathrum:

> *"Resheph would not forgive her trespass. She contracted the*
> *rotting tongue while abetting the lepers of [her region of*
> *exile]. When her time came, she-"*  
>  
> *(The line ends on the dash. The conversation continues in a*
> *separate node:)*  
>  
> *"Resheph would not let her return home."*  
> — *(hidden conversation: Barathrum)*

Zothom's gentler restatement, in the village version: *"she lost
her voice and her life."* The two phrasings sit together. The
rotting tongue is the disease. Losing the voice is its outcome.
The teacher who told her apprentice the orphic truth died unable
to speak. Resheph would not let her return home.

Yla Haj's village-version: *"he excommunicated her from the
sultanate and banished her from the realm."*

Three witnesses, three angles on the same fact. The triangulation
matters because none of them is wrong. The public record is true
as far as it goes. The hidden record extends it. Resheph, the
healer Sultan, the Coiled Lamb — the same figure who had once
asked Rebekah for a thin scan of her mind so he could be three
minds at once — refused to let her come home, and let her die
abetting lepers in a foreign region.

## [VI. WHERE THEY BURIED HER — 15:00]

`[VISUAL: Ezra village. Old stones. Bas-reliefs. The Spindle
visible in the distance.]`

She was buried at Ezra. The exact phrasing in the source — passive,
unattributed — is *"an outsider unfit to be interred with the
honored dead."* The source does not name the deciders. The
script will not name them either. What is known: she was *deemed*
unfit for the honored dead's burial ground; the alternative
chosen was Ezra.

Which turns out, on inspection, to be the more significant
honor.

Two facts about Ezra, both from in-game sources. Yla Haj of the
Daughters:

> *"Ezra sits in the shadow of history, where small things grow.*
> *It's the moss beneath the statue, quite literally. For*
> *centuries monarchs concerned themselves with felling trees, and*
> *now of the Covenate only Ezra remains."*  
> — *(conversation: Yla Haj)*

*Of the Covenate only Ezra remains.* The Covenate — the age when
the Coven held its compact with Earth — left behind one
surviving site, and Ezra is it. The deepest historical layer in
the world.

And the mayor of Ezra is a tree named Haddas, who is himself
ancient. He places his own seed-date "to the reign of [Sultan IV]."
A visitor can point out that this technically makes him an Eater,
and Haddas allows only: *"HA HA HA. I suppose so."* He explains
the place to a visitor:

> *"Ezra is more than tree-old. It's stone-old. Seedlings have*
> *lived here since our roots dreamed of joggling their fingers at*
> *the salt sky."*  
> *"They ate earth and belched freight at the stars, and this*
> *place was their stomach."*  
> *"Later, as the Star-Tree died and they buried their dead under*
> *its roots, this was their funerary place."*  
> — *(conversation: Haddas)*

*Funerary place.* Ezra is, in Haddas's account, the *Eater
funerary site* — where the precursor civilization buried its
dead under the roots of the Star-Tree, which Haddas elsewhere
identifies as the Spindle itself: *"Those who Eat called her
Gjaus, and those who Wake call her Spindle."*

So: Rebekah was denied burial with the Sultanate's honored dead,
and was therefore buried in the *Eater* honored-dead ground —
the burial place of the civilization Barathrum identifies Resheph
as an archon of. She is in the Eaters' grave-soil. The source
does not flag this contrast explicitly; the script raises it
because the two pieces of in-game text — Zothom on her burial
and Haddas on Ezra's history — sit in adjacent corpus files and
together describe one fact.

One more mechanical detail. Per Zothom: *"Only those who pay
their respects to the Grave of Rebekah will know the way into the
tomb."* Rebekah's grave is the literal key into the Tomb of the
Eaters. (Where the path leads from there — Resheph's burial
chamber, the dream-city the game calls Brightsheol, the Spindle's
control unit — is the subject of the next episode; what the corpus
establishes *here* is only that the grave is the entrance.) The
world's geography makes a victim's grave the doorway to the place
the regime's record refuses to discuss.

## [VII. THE DAUGHTERS WHO TINKER — 17:30]

`[VISUAL: a Daughter at work. Tools that are both surgical and
mechanical.]`

Yla Haj, the Daughter who lives at the gravesite, introduces
herself like this:

> *"I am a Daughter of Exile, but you should call me Yla Haj."*  
> *"Tinker-nuns. The world sees fit to call us that, my sisters*
> *and me."*  
> — *(conversation: Yla Haj)*

Tinker-nuns. The order is mostly women, dedicated to Rebekah's
memory and to a vocation that, on its face, doesn't match her —
why would a *healer's* order be an order of *tinkers?*

The question comes up explicitly in the hidden conversation with
Barathrum, in the form of a player-asked line:

> *"Rebekah was a healer. But the daughters of Exile, her*
> *acolytes, are tinkers. Why?"*  
> — *(hidden conversation: Barathrum)*

Barathrum's answer was already quoted in section II, but it
deserves repeating here, in its context: *"She was a healer, yes.
The wire cutter can perform the work of a scalpel; and the
soldering iron, a suture. To take apart a history, and stitch
something new..."*

Yla Haj, asked the same question in her own conversation, gives
a more compressed version: *"You presume too narrow a meaning of
'health'."*

Both answers say the same thing in different registers. The
Daughters are not a contradiction of their founder's vocation.
They are an extension of it. Rebekah's tools were both
surgical and mechanical, and her work — by Barathrum's verb —
was *taking apart a history, and stitching something new* in
its place. The Daughters carry that work forward. The script
will not extrapolate further about what specifically they
build or undo; the source does not narrate the order's
ongoing projects.

`[VISUAL: hold on Yla Haj's face as she works on a mechanism.]`

The order's stated principle is Yla Haj's other line:

> *"The shape of a society is visible only from the outside, and*
> *there is peace beyond a boundary."*

The Daughters choose exile. Rebekah's exile was imposed; theirs
is chosen. They live at the grave of the woman who showed them
how. They pilgrimage. They make tools. They watch.

One detail the source notes but does not explain. In the
Daughters' faction file, their *worship attitudes* are mostly
negative — toward Girsh, toward Entropic, toward the five
Sultan-cults — *with one exception.* Toward Resheph the worship
attitude is *+50.* (Their *feelings* toward the Templar are
separately negative, but that's the rivalry table, not worship.)
The order founded by Rebekah's followers worships her killer.
The source does not say why. It is possible the Daughters do not
know what their founder knew. It is possible they do know and the
worship is something other than what it looks like. The script
will not pretend to answer this. It is one of the corpus's open
questions.

## [VIII. ZOTHOM AND THE QUESTION OF GRIEF — 20:00]

`[VISUAL: Zothom at the grave. Yla Haj passes him without
acknowledgment.]`

There is one more figure at Ezra worth naming. Zothom the
Penitent. A reformed graverobber, by his own account: *"I was
once a vile graverobber, the lowest of the low. Years ago I
sought to plunder the Tomb of the Eaters."* His confederates
died in the attempt; a coterie of mopango sheltered him; he
emerged owing them a debt he is now working off.

> *"I watch over the Grave of Rebekah to atone for my deeds, and*
> *to find my credo."*  
> *"I believe her proximity will help me find my credo."*  
> — *(conversation: Zothom)*

He hopes the grave will give him something. A credo. A meaning.
The Daughters reject this completely. Yla Haj on Zothom:

> *"Zothom. I don't see utility in unkindness, but he's a fool.*
> *Were it that proximity to meekness made one meek..."*  
> *"I have no use for a man who seeks salvation in the body of a*
> *dead woman."*  
> — *(conversation: Yla Haj)*

This is a small detail that says a great deal about the
Daughters' relationship to their founder. Zothom is at the grave
to *take* — to draw meaning from Rebekah's presence. Yla Haj is
at the grave to *witness* — to live alongside it, to maintain
it, to remember without extracting. The Daughters consider the
grave sacred (Yla Haj uses that word explicitly) — but they
consider it sacred in the watchful sense, not the extractive one.
Zothom wants the grave to *give* him something. The Daughters
live with it, maintain it, and keep sight of what its presence
means about the world that put it there.

---

## [IX. A HOLOGRAM AT THE END — 21:30]

`[VISUAL: a fragmented holographic figure. CRT static. One line of
archaic English.]`

There is one more place in the game where Rebekah's name appears.
In `HiddenConversations.xml`, alongside the hidden Barathrum and
hidden Resheph dialogues, there is a conversation called
`RebekahHologram`. It has one node, one line:

> **"Aha, thy phantasm Magicks art crystalsibs to the shivers o'**
> **the skin of Avalon. And, thee? Moon King??"**  
> — *(hidden conversation: Rebekah Hologram)*

A hologram. Speaking in the same archaic compound-word register
that Resheph speaks in at the apex of the Spindle. *Phantasm
Magicks.* *Crystalsibs.* The line ends in a question — *"And,
thee? Moon King??"* — addressing the player, or someone the
hologram believes the player to be.

The game does not, in its extracted corpus, narrate where the
hologram is found or what it is doing there. The conversation
ID lives next to `BarathrumHologram`, `ArchonHologram`, and a
small stack of others in the same hidden file. The script will
not connect this hologram to Resheph's triumvirate thin-scan or
to any other origin point; the source does not establish that
connection. What can be said is only this: somewhere in the
late game, in a form the player can address, a record of Rebekah
exists and speaks. One line. Cryptically. In a register not quite
the Sultan's and not quite anyone else's in the corpus.

## [X. THE QUESTION — 22:30]

`[VISUAL: the Spindle at dusk. Ezra in shadow at its base. Camera
holds.]`

You can climb the Spindle. You can confront Resheph at its apex,
the way Rebekah did. The hidden conversation gives the player a
line directed at his triumvirate composition:

> *"Resheph! If the archon rated nothing of value in the felt and*
> *feeling animal, surely the triad does. You must reconsider."*  
> — *(hidden conversation: Resheph)*

*The triad.* The thin-scanned minds of Rebekah and Barathrum
still encoded in the Seraph's personality. The player asking the
Seraph to listen to *its own component parts* — including the
woman it killed for telling the truth.

The game does not tell you whether the appeal works.

What it tells you is what it always tells you. The Daughters keep
their order. The grave remains at Ezra. Zothom keeps watch. Yla
Haj keeps her boundary. The Sultanate's chronicles still record
Rebekah as an advisor who lost favor. The hidden conversation
file still flags itself
`ExcludeFromCorpusGeneration='true'`. The story Rebekah died
for telling is one the world still mostly doesn't know.

In the next video, we'll talk about the Spindle itself — the
Star-Tree the Eaters called *Gjaus*, the Mark of Death that
opens its sealed tomb, the chamber called Brightsheol the player
must transit to reach its control unit, and the long climb that
the Daughters' founder once made twice: once in service, and
once in betrayal.

`[END CARD.]`
