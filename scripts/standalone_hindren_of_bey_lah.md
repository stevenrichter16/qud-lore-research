# The Hindren of Bey Lah: A Village Built on Fear

**A standalone deep-lore script for Caves of Qud.** Not part of the
Resheph/Spindle series — fully self-contained; no prior episodes
required. Target runtime: 20-23 min · target wordcount: ~3500 words.

**Strict-evidence rule** (per `feedback_qud_script_strict_evidence`):
every lore claim comes from the extracted corpus. Quotes sourced;
inference labeled; player-determined outcomes never stated as a

## [COLD OPEN — 0:00]

`[VISUAL: the flower fields (06_bey_lah/Flower fields.png); a hidden
village of brinestalk yurts (06_bey_lah/Bey lah.png); deer-folk who
flinch at the sight of you.]`

Somewhere in the flower fields of Qud there is a village you are not
supposed to find. Its border scouts will change the landmarks around
you, make "confusing and threatening sounds," and do everything they
can to turn you back without ever being seen. *(book: Fauns of the
Meadow)* If you reach the first neat row of watervine anyway, the
farmers tending it will take *"terrified flight."*

This is Bey Lah, home of the hindren — a few hundred antlered
deer-people who, in the words of one outside scholar, *"do not care
to interact with other creatures when they can avoid it."* And the
reason they avoid you, the reason they hid their village, the reason
they will erase their own children from memory rather than let them
leave — is the single organizing principle of their entire
civilization.

It is fear. This is an episode about a society that took fear and
built a working culture out of it — and about what happens, one day,
when an outsider walks in.

`[TITLE CARD: THE HINDREN OF BEY LAH: A VILLAGE BUILT ON FEAR.]`

## [I. THE DEER-PEOPLE — 1:30]

`[VISUAL: a hindren villager (06_bey_lah/Hindren villager.png) at
work; antlers, fur, a vinereaper.]`

Most of what an outsider can learn about the hindren comes from a
single document: the travel diary of a hunter-scholar named Kaylenn
Sand-Shell, who spent years searching for Bey Lah and finally lived
there a while. *(book: Fauns of the Meadow)* She describes them
plainly:

> *"One can traverse the arable lands around Moghra'yi for lifetimes*
> *without ever encountering the deer-people who call themselves the*
> *'hindren'. In numbers they are but a few hundred."*  
> — *(book: Fauns of the Meadow)*

They are, physically, formidable: *"large, powerful creatures with
arms and legs at least as strong as a human's and covered in dense
fur."* Each carries a sharp metal *vinereaper*; their antlers are
*"strong as bone and naturally quite sharp."* Sand-Shell notes,
almost as a warning, that *"hindren pariahs who learn to fight live
longer than most of Qud's wayfarers."*

So this is the first surprising thing about the hindren. They are
*not* weak. They are big, strong, antlered, and — for all that
"deer-people" might suggest a biped — built more like deer-centaurs:
they *canter* on hooves ("hooves stamp the packed earth," the game
notes elsewhere) below a humanoid, arm-bearing torso. They are
perfectly capable of defending themselves. Their fear is not the
fear of the helpless. It is a choice — a cultural one — and that
makes it far stranger.

## [II. A CULTURE OF FEAR — 4:00]

`[VISUAL: the lah plant / dreadroot; a tumbling pod; the chef Agate
Severance Star (06_bey_lah/Agate severance star.png).]`

The clearest statement of the hindren's defining trait comes from an
unexpected source — a cookbook author. Chef Agate Severance Star,
writing a treatise on the lah plant, pauses to describe the people
who cultivate it:

> *"The very core of hindren culture is an overwhelming fear that*
> *governs every facet of their being. They call outsiders 'kendren'*
> *and refuse to believe that they can be understood. They fear*
> *boars even as they cook and eat boar-flesh. They fear one*
> *another."*  
> — *(book: Blood and Fear)*

Read that again: *they fear one another.* The fear is not only
pointed outward at the world. It is turned inward, at the neighbor,
the elder, the self. And the chef identifies something even more
striking — that the hindren don't merely *feel* fear, they
*cultivate* it:

> *"the most extraordinary incorporation of fear into the hindren*
> *way of life is, without a doubt, the cultivation of fear. I refer*
> *not to the Hindriarch's propaganda or proliferation of*
> *fear-mongering tall tales, though both are mainstays of Bey Lah."*  
> — *(book: Blood and Fear)*

The chef is pointing at the literal crop — the *lah* plant, whose
lifecycle is "steeped in blood and fear" (more on that in a moment).
But notice what the line takes for granted in passing: *the
Hindriarch's propaganda and fear-mongering tall tales are mainstays
of Bey Lah.* The leader of the village manufactures fear on purpose.
We'll come back to why.

Even the children's songs are about it. The faundren — the hindren
young — sing a nursery rhyme that the chef recorded, about two
faundren who ate from their lah field and then forgot to tend it:

> *"Now Elohart and Belahind / lie in a field of lah / because their*
> *fields got hungry / and devoured the faundren raw."*  
> — *(book: Blood and Fear)*

A lullaby whose moral is: do your work, or the world will eat you.

## [III. THE PLANT THAT FEEDS ON FEAR — 7:00]

`[VISUAL: dreadroot in the canyons vs. the cultivated lahbloom;
tumbling pods rolling.]`

It's worth pausing on the lah plant itself, because the hindren's
staple crop is, fittingly, a monster. According to the chef's
treatise, *lah* and the fear-inducing *dreadroot* are the same
plant at different life stages. In the wild canyons, dreadroot is
constantly eaten by baboons and snapjaws before it can mature. But
in the safety of the flower fields, where boars prefer other food,
it *"flourishes, gaining a measure of cognitive process, limited
mobility, and a new means of reproduction."* *(book: Blood and
Fear)*

A mature feral lah does not attack to feed. The chef is precise
about this:

> *"feral lah attacks because it is hungry. It is not... The*
> *advantage of lah's murderous demeanor is not to sustain itself,*
> *but to dramatically speed reproduction of its kind."*  
> — *(book: Blood and Fear)*

It grows *"tumbling pods"* — thorn-covered husks under "tremendous
kinetic tension" that roll along the ground until they hit a living
creature, then detonate, flinging thorns and buds *"just after
making an obnoxious squeaking noise."* If the prey dies, the buds
root in the corpse and grow new lah. *(book: Blood and Fear)*

So the hindren feed themselves, day after meticulous day, on a crop
that is itself a fear-weapon — a flower that kills to reproduce,
tended in tidy rows by a people who fear everything. The chef's
title says it exactly: *blood and fear.* The hindren grow their
defining emotion in the ground and eat it for dinner.

## [IV. THE GRAND-DOE — 10:00]

`[VISUAL: Hindriarch Keh (06_bey_lah/Hindriarch keh.png) under her
"hawkish gaze"; faundren in the background.]`

Bey Lah is, in Sand-Shell's words, ruled by *"informal matriarchal
gerontocratic rule."* The structure is simple:

> *"Their eldest or wisest woman becomes 'Hindriarch', and her word*
> *on most things is obeyed by every member of the tribe, who refer*
> *to her as 'Grand-doe'."*  
> — *(book: Fauns of the Meadow)*

A gerontocracy — rule by the old — selected from among the women,
with near-absolute authority. The hindren's whole naming system,
which Sand-Shell worked out, hangs off this culture. The suffix
*-dren* marks a sentient: *hindren* the people, *faundren* the young,
*kendren* the word for a non-hostile outsider. Personal names end in
*-hind* or *-hart* — and there's a buried bit of social history
there: Sand-Shell reports that *-hart* was once *"a name appendage
reserved for the smallest or most ill-behaved faundren,"* now
treated as unisex. She also heard the term *hartind* — *"an ancient
term for hindren who lived as both genders or neither, which the
young ones take as evidence of a more egalitarian past."*

That detail matters, because the present is not egalitarian. And the
person who embodies the difference is the current Grand-Doe.

## [V. THE ERASED — 12:30]

`[VISUAL: a lone hindren pariah (06_bey_lah/Hindren pariah.png) in
the wastes; a scout (06_bey_lah/Hindren scout.png) at the border.]`

Here is the cruelest machinery of Bey Lah's fear, and the one the
whole present-day story turns on. Anyone who leaves the village —
for any reason — is gone forever:

> *"any of their number who chooses to leave does so permanently and*
> *is erased from the tribe's collective memory... within Bey Lah*
> *itself, pariahs are even more thoroughly ostracized than I knew;*
> *any mention of their names is spoken only in whispers by the*
> *faundren, knowing that being caught speaking of the 'dead' means*
> *harsh punishment."*  
> — *(book: Fauns of the Meadow)*

The *dead.* That is what the hindren call the ones who left. Not the
exiled, not the gone — the dead. To leave Bey Lah is to be
ritually killed in the memory of everyone you ever knew. The
scattered hindren families a traveler might meet anywhere in Qud are
all pariahs — *"whether due to crimes committed or simply the choice
to live elsewhere"* — and not one of them will tell an outsider how
to find the home that erased them.

Sand-Shell saw the cost up close. As she left, a single faundren
snuck out behind her, *"refused to return to their village,"* and
pleaded to come along. She hardened her heart and sent the child
back with a scout. Her closing line about the young one is the whole
tragedy of Bey Lah in a sentence: *"the poor thing does not lack for
bravery."* In a culture built on fear, the child's bravery is the
problem.

## [VI. HINDRIARCH KEH — 15:00]

`[VISUAL: Keh and her enforcer Kesehind (06_bey_lah/Kesehind.png);
the heirloom bracelet Kindrish (06_bey_lah/Kindrish.png).]`

The current Grand-Doe is Hindriarch Keh, and the game lets you meet
her. She greets an outsider — a *kendren* — with wary courtesy and a
job: the village's treasure, an heirloom bracelet called *Kindrish*,
has gone missing, taken (she says) by an exile named Eskhind who
fled the village weeks ago. Because no hindren may leave to retrieve
it without violating their codes, Keh needs an outsider. Her
instructions are chilling in their casualness:

> *"Find the exile called Eskhind and retrieve Kindrish from her,*
> *even... even if the cost is the life of her and her siblings."*  
> — *(conversation: Hindriarch Keh)*

Two details mark Keh as more than a frightened elder. First, her
attitude toward the young. Whenever the player lingers, she frets
that the outsider's mere presence is dangerous: *"I don't want the
faundren to become used to our borders being permeable,"* and later,
*"try not to give the faundren any ideas."* The thing she is most
afraid of is her own children's curiosity.

Second — and this is the detail that reveals her — Keh refuses to
recognize the gender of one of Eskhind's siblings. Pressed on it,
she invokes the hartind tradition only to weaponize it:

> *"Our people have a long history of two-gender hindren called*
> *hartind, respected and consecrated. There is a proper path to*
> *gender variance in our society, of which the male exile could*
> *have availed himself years ago. He never did, so I do not*
> *recognize him."*  
> — *(conversation: Hindriarch Keh)*

The "more egalitarian past" the faundren dream about has become, in
Keh's mouth, a bureaucratic gate she uses to deny who someone is.
Eskhind rejects the framing outright — *"I have no brother. My two
sisters are all the blood family I can claim"* — so the "male exile"
Keh refuses to recognize is, to Eskhind, simply her sister. The
Grand-Doe is not just fearful. She is a fearful ruler who has
learned to use the village's fear as a tool.

## [VII. THE FRAME-UP — 18:00]

`[VISUAL: Eskhind the exile (06_bey_lah/Eskhind.png); Angohind the
aspiring detective (06_bey_lah/Angohind.png); Warden Neelahind
(06_bey_lah/Warden neelahind.png).]`

When you find Eskhind, hidden with her siblings in a hollowed-out
tree, she tells a different story. She is, she says, *"falsely
accused"* — and across the conversation she indicts the whole
system. In one exchange:

> *"Bey Lah's isolationism provides ideal patsies for any crime the*
> *Hindriarch chooses to commit."*  
> — *(conversation: Eskhind)*

And, asked to defend the claim, she traces it to the structure
itself:

> *"The gerontocracy ensures that its leaders will always be aging*
> *and fearful. Pariahs like me can be easily accused of crimes*
> *committed by Keh or her cronies."*  
> — *(conversation: Eskhind)*

This is the genius of the episode's writing: the village's defining
fear is also the perfect cover for corruption. A culture that erases
its exiles and forbids anyone to question the Grand-Doe is a culture
where the powerful can pin any crime on the already-erased. Eskhind
doesn't even ask the outsider to believe her over Keh — only for
something fairer: *"extend to me the same level of trust you give
Keh. Is that not reasonable? Is that not just?"*

What she asks for instead of faith is an *investigation*. *"The
proof is in the petals, kendren. You want justice? Find out what
really happened."* And the village, it turns out, has someone eager
to help: Angohind, a young hindren who introduces himself as *"an
aspirant investigator with an ear... for mystery,"* and who can feel
that *"something strange is afoot here in Bey Lah, and no mistake."*
The arbiter is Warden Neelahind — by Eskhind's account *"loyal to
Grand-Doe, but more loyal to the truth."* The player gathers the
evidence; Neelahind delivers the verdict. The quest's own summary
names the target plainly: the investigation acts *"against thieves
and the authorities who empower them."* *(quest: Kith and Kin)*

A society this afraid of outsiders ends up needing one — a *kendren*
— to do the single thing none of its frightened insiders can: ask a
question out loud.

## [VIII. LOVE, AND THE WINDS OF CHANGE — 20:30]

`[VISUAL: two hindren in a tree-stump; faundren peering past the
border at the wider world.]`

Underneath the mystery is a love story. Hindriarch Keh, minimizing,
calls Eskhind and Warden Neelahind *"close friends as children."*
Eskhind's own account is plainer and deeper: *"We were close. More
than close... leaving Neelahind shredded it into scraps."* The game
gives the player the chance to do more than clear a name. In the quest *Love and Fear*, you can recover a
discarded love sonnet and reunite them; one telling of the outcome
has the player *"marrying Eskhind and the Warden Neelahind"* in
*"the burnt out core of a tree stump."* *(quest: Love and Fear)* A
poem, in a village whose Hindriarch *"detest[s] poetry"* for its
*"purposefully unclear language."* The fearful ruler has no use for
the one form of speech that admits it doesn't have all the answers.

And the future is already pressing on Bey Lah's walls. Sand-Shell
saw it years before: the faundren's *"curiosity about the outside
world is rapidly becoming difficult for the elders to contain... the
winds of change are coming to Bey Lah."* Depending on what the player
does, those winds blow different ways. In some outcomes the borders
open and the village *prospers* — one villager marvels, *"How Bey
Lah prospers! We should have opened the village years ago"* — and a
newly-arrived people, the slynth, can even be voted in as permanent
residents. In others, it goes badly: the game tracks states where
the village is *"ravaged"* or *"doomed,"* where Keh mourns that it is
*"all gone."* *(conversation: Hindriarch Keh)* The script will not
pretend there is one canonical ending. The game hands the fate of
the meadow to the player, exactly as the *Petals on the Wind* quest
says — *"meddle in the affairs of the meadow at the behest of a
deerfolk pariah."*

---

## [IX. THE QUESTION — 22:00]

`[VISUAL: Bey Lah at dusk (06_bey_lah/Bey lah.png); a faundren at the
edge of the field, looking out; hold.]`

Bey Lah is one of the smallest stories in Caves of Qud — a few
hundred deer-folk, a stolen bracelet, a village you can walk through
in an afternoon. But it is one of the most complete arguments the
game makes about how a society works.

The hindren built something real with their fear. They survived,
peacefully, for generations, hidden in the flower fields, while the
rest of Qud bled. Their isolation kept them safe. Their solidarity
kept them fed. Their refusal to be understood kept them whole. The
fear *worked* — Sand-Shell, no admirer of the place, admitted she
*"cannot argue against its efficacy."*

And the cost of it is every name spoken only in a whisper. Every
faundren who wanted to see the world and was called dead for it.
Every crime a frightened ruler could pin on the already-erased.
Every poem detested for asking a question. A culture built on fear
buys safety with everything that might have grown — the curious
child, the returning exile, the lovers kept apart, the simple act of
asking out loud what really happened.

The winds of change are coming to Bey Lah. The game lets you decide
whether you are the storm that ravages it or the looking-glass that
finally lets it see itself — and it does not tell you which one a
village like this deserves. That, like the proof, is in the petals.

`[END CARD.]`

**Tone reminders:**
- STANDALONE. Do not reference Resheph, the Spindle, Barathrum, the
  Coda, or anything from the main series. The episode must stand
  alone. (The corpus has incidental "Resheph"-as-oath lines and a
  "shadow of the spindle" image in Bey Lah texts — deliberately NOT
  used, to keep this self-contained.)
- The thesis is FEAR as a functioning social technology — present it
  even-handedly. The hindren's fear *works* (Sand-Shell concedes its
  efficacy); the episode's power is in the cost, not in mocking them.
- Keh is a fearful ruler, not a cartoon villain. Her gender-denial
  and her framing of exiles are the sharp edges; play them straight.
- Player-determined outcomes (village prospers / ravaged / doomed;
  lovers married or not; borders open or not) must be presented as a
  menu, never a single canon. §VIII and §IX both flag this.
- "The proof is in the petals" is Eskhind's line; it makes a good
  recurring motif and closer. Use it.

**Why this works as a one-off:**
- Self-contained quest cluster (Petals on the Wind → Kith and Kin →
  Find Eskhind → Love and Fear), unconnected to the main quest.
- A single strong thesis (fear as culture) a newcomer can grasp cold.
- A complete cast (tyrant, exile, detective, warden, lovers) and a
  complete arc (mystery → justice → change) in one location.
