# Joppa: The People of the Vine

**A standalone deep-lore script for Caves of Qud.** A portrait of a
single village — the small, ordinary place at the edge of the world.
Not part of the Resheph/Spindle series and distinct from the faction
and entity one-offs. Self-contained; no prior episodes required.
Target runtime: 21-24 min · target wordcount: ~3500 words.

**Strict-evidence rule** (per `feedback_qud_script_strict_evidence`):
every lore claim comes from the extracted corpus. Quotes sourced;
inference labeled; player-determined outcomes never stated as a
single canon. The Caves of Qud wiki is used ONLY for the downloaded
visual assets at the end — never as a lore source.

## [COLD OPEN — 0:00]

`[VISUAL: the Joppa overworld tile — a few huts in a green marsh
hemmed by salt; then the village interior at dusk.]`

In a game about dead god-kings, sentient cities, and the end of the
world, there is a small village where you can sit down on a pillow in
the shade, and an old man will offer you a drink of water.

> *"Live and drink. Come in- come sit 'neath the cool shade, 'cross a*
> *pillow there. And welcome to Joppa. You may drink of our*
> *freshwater, too, and quench your thirst."*  
> — *(conversation: Irudad)*

This is Joppa. It is a watervine farm in a salt marsh, with a handful
of residents, no walls to speak of, and almost nothing that anyone
from the wider world would call important. And it is the most
important place in Caves of Qud — because it is the place all of that
wider world is *for.*

This is an episode about the smallest thing the game contains: an
ordinary village, the people of the vine, and what it costs to keep
an ordinary life alive in a world that is trying very hard to end it.

`[TITLE CARD: JOPPA — THE PEOPLE OF THE VINE.]`

## [I. THE CRACK BETWEEN THE SALT AND THE JUNGLE — 1:30]

`[VISUAL: a map-pull — the Great Salt Desert to the west, the jungle
to the east, Joppa's green marsh in the seam between them.]`

Ask the village elder where, exactly, you are, and he gives you the
geography in a single breath:

> *"The oasis-hamlet. 'Neath the shelf of the world. A million*
> *breaths of salt the wind heaves over the Great Salt Desert*
> *Moghra'yi. And to the east, the rotting jungles of Qud. Here in*
> *the crack between the two, watervine can grow, and we grow it."*  
> — *(conversation: Irudad)*

That is the whole proposition of Joppa, in three sentences. On one
side, a salt desert called Moghra'yi so vast the wind carries "a
million breaths of salt" off it. On the other, rotting jungle. And in
the narrow seam between the killing dryness and the killing rot,
there is exactly enough water for a crop to grow — so the village
grows it.

How exactly does anything live in that seam? A farmer named Mehmet —
who reads the world by *taste,* and will casually tell you he can
detect lime, and maybe gallium, on the breeze — explains the margin
the village survives on:

> *"Wind off the Great Desert cools a touch and gives up moisture.*
> *Just enough. A tongue can taste a hundred kinds of wind, y'know?"*  
> — *(conversation: Mehmet)*

*Just enough.* That phrase is the entire ecology of Joppa. The desert
that should kill them instead exhales a thread of moisture as it
cools, and that thread is the difference between a village and a
graveyard. What they do with it is grow watervine — the staple crop
of this whole region, the plant you wring for drinking water. As
Mehmet puts it, with a farmer's flat pride: *"Watervine farm in the
lap of the marsh. You've sucked the moisture out a vinewafer, yea? We
tend the plant here."* *(conversation: Mehmet)*

That's it. That's the job. In a world of artifact-hunters and
god-machines, the people of Joppa tend a plant in a marsh, in the one
crack of the map where a plant can be tended.

## [II. THE PEOPLE OF THE VINE — 4:30]

`[VISUAL: watervine paddies; a vinereaper blade; villagers at a
festival table.]`

Joppa keeps two slim volumes of its own history, and between them
they tell you everything about how this village sees itself. The
first records the founding — not of a kingdom, but of an identity:

> *"In the mystic air of Jeweled Dusk, a circle of farmers clasped*
> *hands and sheathed a vinereaper in the moistened soil. From then*
> *on, the villagers of Joppa were known as the people of the vine."*  
> — *(book: History of Joppa, Vol. 1)*

There is no conquest in Joppa's founding myth, no king, no war. There
is a circle of farmers, holding hands, planting a *vinereaper* — the
curved blade you harvest watervine with — into wet soil. The founding
act of the village is the decision to *farm.* From that gesture, they
take their name: the people of the vine.

The second volume is even smaller, and it's about *dinner:*

> *"Since the first festival of Ut yara Ux, the villagers of Joppa*
> *have feasted on warm apple matz."*  
> — *(book: History of Joppa, Vol. 2)*

Ut yara Ux is a festival kept across Qud; what's recorded as Joppa's
own is the food — *warm apple matz,* a dish made from matz, the
flatbread-staple of the region. A whole volume of the village history
is devoted to the fact that, at the festival, they eat warm apple
bread. That is not a joke at Joppa's expense. It is the point. When
your history books are about the day you decided to farm and the
dish you serve at the holiday, it means nothing catastrophic has ever
been important enough to crowd them out. A peaceful history is a
*short* one.

And running underneath all of it is the thing every traveler in Qud
says and Joppa means most literally: *"Live and drink."* It is hello.
It is goodbye. It is a blessing. In a world where water is survival,
the universal greeting is a toast to staying alive — and in Joppa,
where they grow the water, the elder backs the words with the act,
offering you his *"freshwater"* before you've asked for anything at
all.

## [III. THE ELDER AND HIS DAUGHTER — 8:00]

`[VISUAL: Elder Irudad on his pillow; then Nima Ruda among drying
herbs.]`

The center of Joppa is its elder, Irudad — host, memory-keeper, and
the man who examines whatever the wider world drags to his door. He
is gentle and slow, all *"-mm"*s and pauses, and his picture of the
world beyond the marsh is strikingly clear-eyed for a man who never
leaves it:

> *"There are people, friends, communities. And friends-to-noone,*
> *too. History is thick as the high salt sun, and where the past is*
> *ground up like matz meal, a mix of life sets in."*  
> — *(conversation: Irudad)*

He knows precisely what's out there — wonders and predators both —
and he watches the young leave for it without illusion. The
adventurers who set off from villages like his, he says, go chasing
*"their own splinter of artifact. To both Fates, life and death."*
*(conversation: Irudad)* *Both Fates.* He has clearly watched people
walk north out of Joppa and not come back, and he names it plainly:
some find a relic and a story, some find a grave, and the village
elder loves them anyway and lets them go.

Irudad has a daughter, and she is one of the quietest, most human
characters in the game. Her name is Nima Ruda, and she runs the
village apothecary:

> *"I grew up right here in Joppa, daughter of the Elder and all.*
> *Spent some years delving, but gave it up after a few near escapes*
> *and nearly deadly wounds. I'm better suited to herbalism anyway,*
> *and my father worries less."*  
> — *(conversation: Nima Ruda)*

Read that carefully, because it is a complete life in four sentences.
Nima Ruda is the protagonist of a different game — the one where the
elder's child goes "delving" into the ruins for artifacts and glory.
She *tried* it. And she came back, because she nearly died, and
because she is "better suited to herbalism," and because it makes her
father worry less. Her shop stocks exactly what a marsh village
needs: *"Witchwood for pain, bandages for bleeding, yuckwheat and
honey for when your guts act up."* *(conversation: Nima Ruda)*

And here is the detail that makes her unforgettable. Ask Nima Ruda if
she'll inherit Joppa from her father — become the next elder — and
she recoils from it:

> *"Fates forfend. I would hardly like to lead a village and run a*
> *shop at the same time."*  
> — *(conversation: Nima Ruda)*

No dynasty. No destiny. The elder's daughter does not want the
throne, because Joppa does not have a throne — it has a job, two
jobs, actually, and she already has one she likes. This is what
power looks like in an ordinary place: a thing nobody is fighting
over, a responsibility, declined with a shrug.

## [IV. THE WARDEN AND THE WANDERER — 12:00]

`[VISUAL: Warden Yrame at the village edge; then Tam among his chests
of drawers.]`

Even the smallest village in Qud keeps someone to kill its monsters.
Joppa's is Warden Yrame, and Yrame's entire personality fits on a
knife:

> *"Welcome a-Joppa, friend. Stay peacelike, and we stay friends,*
> *hyeh? You make trouble, it's my work a-split you open like a*
> *croc."*  
> — *(conversation: Warden Yrame)*

Yrame is blunt to the point of poetry — *"Called Yrame, Warden
a-Joppa. I kill trouble. Don't be trouble."* — and explains the role
without a flicker of self-importance: *"Nigh every settlement a-Qud
keeps a Warden. We snuff threats to the peace."* *(conversation:
Warden Yrame)* This is the scale of heroism Joppa runs on. Not a
chosen one, not an army — one tough person at the edge of town whose
whole creed is *don't be trouble, and if you are, I'll handle it.*
The peace of the world, in Joppa, is kept one croc-split at a time.

And then there is Tam, who is not from here at all. Tam is a *dromad*
— a saltstrider, one of the camel-like caravan-folk of the deep
desert — and Tam's story is the gentlest argument the village makes
for itself:

> *"I walked Moghra'yi in the caravans of my brethren and the*
> *saltback, but upon meeting Elder Irudad, knew at once to settle*
> *down here."*  
> — *(conversation: Tam)*

Think about what that means. Tam's people, by Tam's own account,
*"have walked the salt for thousands of years, meeting every creature*
*that lives and thinks. From Pale Sea to the marsh of Joppa, and*
*under the Hanging Hills."* *(conversation: Tam)* A member of a folk
who have crossed the entire world, over and over, for millennia — and
who, after all of that, met one old farmer at one marsh and *stopped
walking.* Joppa is not just where the locals were born. It is a place
that someone who had seen everything chose, on purpose, as the place
worth staying.

## [V. THE BATTLE OF THE VEGETABLE — 15:30]

`[VISUAL: gnawed watervine; the trek north to Red Rock's red shale;
the fight.]`

Every village has the day the world reached in and touched it. For
Joppa, it begins, fittingly, with the crop. Something starts eating
the watervine. And it's Mehmet — the man who tastes the wind — who
notices, the same way he notices everything: by flavor.

> *"Somethin' is eating our watervine, 'round Beetle Moon. Q-ruun*
> *says he saw a creature, spider-like, slinking in the brine... I*
> *tasted a bit of red fleck in the pool. Shale, like we find in the*
> *soil by Red Rock."*  
> — *(conversation: Mehmet)*

It is the most Joppa mystery imaginable. The crisis is *the crop is
being nibbled,* and the detective work is *the farmer tasted red rock
dust in the water.* The trail leads two parasangs north, to a place
called Red Rock — and what's found there is not a cave pest at all.
It's a *girshling:* a pale, spider-like thing that the elder, when a
corpse is finally laid in front of him, identifies with grim
recognition as a *"creature of plague."* *(conversation: Irudad)*

What follows is the closest thing Joppa has to an epic, and the
game's own chronicle does not undersell it. In the records of the
deed, the wayfarer who took up the task *"walked below the chrome
arches, beat back the throng of girshlings, and healed the wilting
crops"* — and the celebratory version names it outright: *"At the
bloody Battle of Red Rock, [the hero] fought the combined girshling
and baboon forces to liberate the villagers of Joppa."* *(quest:
What's Eating the Watervine?)*

Sit with the proportions of that. A *bloody battle,* fought against a
*throng* of monsters, to *liberate* a village — and the stakes are a
vegetable patch. To anyone outside Joppa, it is a footnote. To Joppa,
it is survival, because the vegetable patch *is* the village; the
watervine is the water is the life. Caves of Qud quietly insists that
this is a real epic — that fighting and bleeding to keep a marsh
hamlet's crop alive is exactly as heroic as anything that happens at
the top of the world. The scale is small. The stakes are total. That
is what an ordinary place's great war looks like.

(Whether *you* are the one who marches to Red Rock is, of course,
yours to decide — this is the first task the village ever asks of a
newcomer, and the records above are how Joppa remembers it when the
crop is saved.)

## [VI. THE OPEN DOOR — 18:30]

`[VISUAL: a group of luminous newcomers at the marsh's edge; Irudad
weighing the question.]`

The last thing to understand about Joppa is what it does when the
world sends it not a monster, but a *need.* At one point a band of
displaced folk — luminous wanderers who've lost their home — go
looking for somewhere to take them in, and the question reaches the
elder: *will Joppa, a poor farming village with barely enough water
for itself, open its door?*

Irudad's answer, if he gives the welcoming one, is the moral center
of the whole village:

> *"Any friend of yours is too a friend of Joppa, and if those*
> *friends desire shelter I wouldn't dare turn them away. I only hope*
> *these lilypad-folk have the patience for a farmer's life."*  
> — *(conversation: Irudad)*

*I wouldn't dare turn them away.* From a man who has just told you his
people are poor farmers who can do little but sharpen their
vinereapers, that is an enormous thing to say. But it is genuinely a
hard question, and the game does not pretend otherwise — the elder
can also, gently, decline: *"I mean no slight to these slynth, but it
is too great a favor you ask of the people of Joppa."* *(conversation:
Irudad)* Whether the door opens is the player's call, and the game
respects how heavy the ask is on a village living on *just enough.*

And if the door does open, the result is not a fairy-tale. Some of
the newcomers can't take the quiet, and leave; others stay and root.
The elder describes it without sentimentality: *"Some cared not for
the slow pace of our lives and struck out on their own... others are
welcome to grow and flourish here in our marshes."* *(conversation:
Irudad)* That is what real welcome looks like — not a guarantee that
everyone belongs, but an open door, and a marsh with room in it for
whoever wants the slow life. Joppa absorbs strangers the same way it
took in Tam: one at a time, by choice, into a life that is mostly
tending plants.

## [VII. THE PLACE WORTH SAVING — 21:00]

`[VISUAL: dusk over the paddies; the elder on his pillow; a new
traveler arriving on the path. Hold.]`

Caves of Qud is a game obsessed with bigness — deep time, dead
empires, the machinery of the cosmos. Joppa is the small thing it
keeps at the bottom of all that, on purpose. It is where every life
in the game begins, blinking, as a nobody; and it never once acts
like it knows it's a beginning. To Joppa, Joppa is not a tutorial. It
is *home* — the center of the only world its people need.

What's remarkable is how *complete* a place it is at this scale. It
has a geography that should be impossible and isn't. It has a
founding myth about deciding to farm and a history book about
holiday bread. It has an elder who loves the children who leave, a
daughter who chose the quiet life and won't take the crown, a dreamer
who tastes the wind, a warden who kills trouble, and a world-walker
who finally stopped walking. It fights a desperate war over a
vegetable and counts it a victory. It opens its door to refugees on
*just enough* water, and means it.

The elder says it best, and he says it as an apology: *"We are poor
farmers, and sharpen our vinereapers is all we can do. But others?
Perhaps more."* *(conversation: Irudad)* He thinks Joppa is the small
thing and the heroes are elsewhere. He has it exactly backwards. The
sultans and the god-machines and the artifact-hunters at the top of
the world — all of that *cosmic struggle is in service of this:* a
crack in the salt where a vine can grow, and a few people who tend
it, and offer you their freshwater, and ask only that you live, and
drink.

`[END CARD.]`

**Why this works as a standalone (and as a companion piece):**
- A single, graspable hook (the smallest village in a vast world) and
  a complete arc (geography → identity → people → their one war →
  their open door → why it matters) in one place.
- No dependency on the main quest; Joppa's residents, history, crop,
  and crises are entirely self-contained.
- Across the standalones, Joppa is the deliberate counterweight: Bey
  Lah, the mopango, the Putus Templar, and Chavvah are extraordinary
  societies; Joppa is the ordinary one — the human baseline that makes
  the strangeness of the others legible. (Keep this cross-set framing
  in the PLAN/notes only; each episode must stand alone.)
