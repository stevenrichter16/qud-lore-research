# The Putus Templar: Ordered Violence

**A standalone deep-lore script for Caves of Qud.** A faction profile,
not part of the Resheph/Spindle series. Self-contained; no prior
episodes required. Target runtime: 21-24 min · target wordcount:
~3500 words.

## [COLD OPEN — 0:00]

`[VISUAL: a small, eager figure looking up off-screen; CRT-soft. No
Domesticant tile exists on the wiki — use a text overlay of the four
lines over a squire/captive tile.]`

If you read the right object in Caves of Qud, you find a page with
four lines on it. They are the entire recorded speech of a creature
called a Templar Domesticant, and they go like this:

> *"Did I do well, Newfather?"*  
> *"Newfather! Look!"*  
> *"Are you proud, Newfather?"*  
> *"Newfather!"*  
> — *(book/conversation: Templar Domesticant)*

That is all it says. A servant, calling for a "Newfather," desperate
to know if it did well. To understand who the Newfather is — and what
was done to this creature to make it speak only in those four
sentences — you have to understand the Putus Templar: an order of
knights who took cruelty, ranked it, ritualized it, and made it holy.

This is an episode about a faction that codified violence into
orthodoxy — and about the children it makes, and the rare one who
gets out.

`[TITLE CARD: THE PUTUS TEMPLAR: ORDERED VIOLENCE.]`

## [I. THE SONS AND DAUGHTERS — 1:30]

`[VISUAL: armored knights; a shield bearing a holy rhombus.]`

The Putus Templar do not call themselves Templar. In the words of
one wary observer, *"The Putus Templar, or the Sons and Daughters,
as they call themselves, are an order of knights who claim
descendancy from the Eaters, the ancient folk who wrought the chrome
halls of Qud."* *(conversation: Otho)*

Two things to hold from that one sentence. First, they are *knights*
— an armored military order, not a mob. Second, they *claim* descent
from the Eaters, the prestigious vanished civilization that built the
old chrome ruins. Their entire legitimacy rests on a bloodline they
say runs back to the makers of the world. Whether the claim is true
matters less than what they do with it: they treat their lineage as
the thing that makes them holy, and everyone outside it as lesser.

You can see their faith in their gear. The Templar carry a shield
called an Aegis, and a real one *"would bear the holy rhombus,"* the
order's sacred symbol. *(conversation: Une)* A blank Aegis — one with
no rhombus — is, as one former associate puts it, something *"no
knight would be caught dead carrying."* The rhombus is the mark of
belonging. To carry the shield without it is to be no one.

And their posture toward the rest of Qud is simple and total. The
observer's warning continues: *"Beware them, for while they despise
all mutants, they harbor a special malice"* for their enemies — and,
he adds, *"Take care that you do not find [yourself] inside one of
their slave pens."* *(conversation: Otho)*

Slave pens. Hold that, too.

## [II. THE THREE COMMANDMENTS — 4:00]

`[VISUAL: a Templar outpost; carved orthodoxy.]`

A traveling scholar named Kaylenn Sand-Shell, who recorded the
customs of many of Qud's peoples, set down the Putus Templar's core
beliefs. In their outposts, she wrote, *"the order's core values
remain immutable as anywhere else"* — and she names three:

> *"dedication to a family tree as tall and unbranching as Qud's*
> *Spindle, complete subjugation of those they consider lesser, and*
> *the total eradication of mutant sapience."*  
> — *(book: The Murmurs' Prayer)*

Three commandments, and they fit together into a closed logic:

1. **Purity of blood.** A family tree *"tall and unbranching"* — no
   marrying out, no mixing, a lineage kept straight as the tallest
   structure in the world.
2. **Subjugation of the lesser.** Everyone not of the blood exists to
   be ruled.
3. **Eradication of mutants.** In a world where mutation is
   everywhere, the Templar's project is to *erase* it — "the total
   eradication of mutant sapience."

This is a purity cult with a body count built into its catechism. And
crucially, Sand-Shell notes that while the *core* is "immutable," the
*"cultural trappings around that core belief"* drift in the border
colonies — which is where the Templar get genuinely strange, and
genuinely horrifying. We'll get there.

## [III. ORDERED VIOLENCE — 7:00]

`[VISUAL: two poles diagrammed — chaos vs. rigid hierarchy.]`

The sharpest analysis of the Putus Templar in the game comes from a
scholar named Q Girl, in a treatise called *From Entropy to
Hierarchy.* She frames the world's threats to social equality as two
opposite poles, and gives each a poster-faction. One pole is pure
chaos — the Gyre wights, *"exponents of senseless violence."* The
other pole is the Templar:

> *"The Templar on the other hand are enactors of extremely ordered*
> *violence. Their brutality has been codified into orthodoxy. Their*
> *ideological structures are anti-entropic but destructive and*
> *abhorrent. They seek to construct a rigidly hierarchical society*
> *with a slave caste on the bottom rung."*  
> — *(book: From Entropy to Hierarchy)*

This is the thesis of the whole faction, and it's worth slowing down
on. The horror of the Putus Templar is not that they are violent.
Plenty of things in Qud are violent. The horror is that their
violence is *ordered* — *"codified into orthodoxy."* It has rules,
ranks, rituals, holy days. It is administered, not unleashed. Q Girl's
word is exactly right: the Templar are *anti-entropic.* They are the
enemies of disorder, and they have decided that the way to defeat
disorder is a society shaped like a ladder with a slave caste welded
to the bottom rung.

And Q Girl names the mechanism by which the ladder reproduces itself:

> *"Through rituals like the baptism in the blood of the reclaimed,*
> *they indoctrinate the privileged among their subjects. The rest of*
> *us are met only with force."*  
> — *(book: From Entropy to Hierarchy)*

*Baptism in the blood of the reclaimed.* Two of the Templar's
ugliest words sit in that phrase. *Reclaimed.* And the ritual that
turns the reclaimed into something useful.

## [IV. THE RECLAIMED — 11:00]

`[VISUAL: back to the Domesticant; the "Newfather" lines on screen.]`

Now we can return to the creature from the cold open. The Templar's
vocabulary draws a hard line between the *reclaimed* and the
*unreclaimed* — between those folded into the order and the rest of
humanity, who are, in the festival diptych's phrase, mere *"detritus
of the unreclaimed."* And Q Girl told us the order *"indoctrinate[s]
the privileged among their subjects"* through ritual. The Templar
Domesticant is what that indoctrination can produce. A *domesticant*
— the word means a domesticated servant — whose entire surviving
speech is four lines of yearning for the approval of a "Newfather":

> *"Did I do well, Newfather? ... Are you proud, Newfather?"*  
> — *(book/conversation: Templar Domesticant)*

*Newfather.* Not father — *new*father. The word does the work. This
is a being that addresses a Templar as the father at the center of
its life and exists, as far as its four lines ever reveal, only to
please him. It does not rage. It does not grieve. It asks if it did
well. The script won't claim more about how a domesticant is made
than the game shows — but the word *domesticated*, applied to a
person, is its own small horror.

This is the quiet genius of the Templar's cruelty, and why Q Girl
calls it *ordered.* The slave pens and the eradication of mutants are
the violence you can see. The Domesticant is the violence you can't —
a person hollowed out and refilled with devotion, who would tell you,
if it could say anything else, that it is happy.

## [V. THE MURMURS' FESTIVAL — 14:30]

`[VISUAL: a grotesque festival — figures in trash capes and bone
helms, made to play music.]`

Here is where Sand-Shell's note about "cultural trappings" pays off,
and where the Templar reveal that their cruelty doesn't only point
outward at mutants and slaves — it points *inward*, at their own.

In the border colonies — Sand-Shell found it in *"the ruins of
Oudin's abandoned outposts North of the Sunderlies"* — the Templar
developed a holiday. She calls it the Murmurs' Festival, and its
function is plain: *"a new holy day of merriment and punishment to
keep the flock pliant."* *(book: The Murmurs' Prayer)* Here is how it
worked:

> *"The least favored of an outpost's squires, so it is said, would*
> *be subdued the evening before the Festival, forced upon the*
> *morning into a costume meant to be a mockery of the imagined*
> *mutant threat, and bade to entertain their might-determined*
> *betters. Upon the festival's conclusion, the crowd's most hated*
> *Murmurs would be 'paid' with public humiliation, beatings, and*
> *possibly torture."*  
> — *(book: The Murmurs' Prayer)*

Read that carefully. The victims of the Murmurs' Festival are not
outsiders. They are the Templar's *own lowest-ranked squires* — their
own children, in effect — dressed up as the very mutants the order
exists to exterminate, made to caper and play music, and then beaten
or tortured for sport. The uniform was fixed: *"trash cape, bone helm,
and musical instrument."*

Sand-Shell found the festival's liturgy preserved in baked clay — a
diptych addressed to the Murmurs themselves. It is one of the most
chilling pieces of text in the game:

> *"You, least among the digits at the end of Oudin's mighty arm,*
> *servants most faithless and failing. Tonight you are no Sons and*
> *Daughters, but only humanity's dying Murmurs. Doff your tabards,*
> *now, and carry instead the detritus of the unreclaimed over your*
> *back! ... Take up an instrument, now, and play us a tune! If you*
> *Murmurs do not please, you will be paid most painfully."*  
> — *(book: The Murmurs' Prayer)*

*Tonight you are no Sons and Daughters.* For one night, the lowest
Templar are stripped of the holy rhombus and made to *be* the thing
the order hates — to wear "the detritus of the unreclaimed," to be
the mutant, the murmur, humanity's dying noise. It is a machine for
manufacturing loyalty out of terror: every squire learns exactly what
waits at the bottom of the ladder, and clings all the harder to their
rung. *Oudin* — named in the diptych as the owner of the "mighty
arm" at whose far end these squires are the lowest "digits," and
elsewhere as the holder of the outposts themselves — is the distant
authority the whole structure answers to. (The corpus names Oudin
only through these possessives; what exactly Oudin *is* — a person, a
seat of power, a stronghold — it leaves unsaid, and so will the
script.) The festival is how that service is kept pliant.

## [VI. THE ONE WHO GOT OUT — 18:00]

`[VISUAL: a masked figure at a peaceful settlement, playing a
hurdy-gurdy; a blank shield leans nearby.]`

There is, in a far corner of Qud, a settlement called the Yd Freehold
— *"a collective thriving without hierarchy,"* in the words of one of
its defenders. That defender is a masked, cybernetically-augmented
figure named Une, who plays an old music machine called a
hurdy-gurdy and answers almost no questions about eir past.

But the game leaves clues, and they point one direction. Une carries
a shield that a traveler might mistake for *"a Templar's Aegis"* — and
when pressed, Une's answer is precise: *"If it were a Templar's Aegis,
it would bear the holy rhombus. No knight would be caught dead
carrying this blank slate."* *(conversation: Une)* A Templar shield
with the holy rhombus scraped off. Une speaks of a former home —
*"my home before was worse than most"* — and of why the Freehold
matters: *"the Freehold is the only welcoming home I could ever have
imagined for myself... we are a collective thriving without hierarchy.
I'm rightly proud to defend that."* *(conversation: Une)*

The game never states outright that Une is a former Putus Templar.
But put the pieces together — the Aegis with its holy mark removed, a
prior home "worse than most," and a fierce devotion to a society
*without hierarchy*, the exact opposite of everything the Templar
build — and the conclusion is hard to avoid. And then there is the
hurdy-gurdy. Recall the Murmurs' uniform: trash cape, bone helm, and
*a musical instrument.* A Murmur was made to play music as
humiliation. Une, free, plays music by choice, and when a rude
visitor says the instrument *"sounds like a dying animal,"* Une's
reply is a whole philosophy: *"You are a dying animal. So am I. We
are all dying animals... We all decay, is the point."* *(conversation:
Une)*

Whatever the Templar tried to make of Une, it did not take. The
script will not assert more than the game does — but the shape of an
escape is right there in the blank shield and the chosen song.

## [VII. THE QUESTION — 21:30]

`[VISUAL: a Templar Aegis with its rhombus; slow pull back; hold.]`

The Putus Templar are, mechanically, just an enemy faction — knights
in chrome who attack on sight, whose initial disposition toward the
player is about as hostile as the game allows. You will most likely
meet them as a wall of armored bodies between you and somewhere you
need to go. It would be easy to file them under "evil knights" and
move on.

But Q Girl's treatise is asking you not to. The reason she sets the
Templar opposite the Gyre wights — ordered violence against chaotic
violence — is that the two are *not* the same evil, and the Templar's
is the more dangerous kind. Chaos burns out. Orthodoxy *reproduces.*
The Gyre wights smash things and scatter. The Templar build a ladder,
write a catechism, hold a festival, and raise the next generation to
maintain all three. Their cruelty has *infrastructure.* It has a holy
symbol, a baptism, a slave caste, a scapegoat ritual, and a word —
*reclamation* — for turning a captured person into a Domesticant who
asks only whether it did well.

And against all of that, the game offers one quiet rebuttal, and it
isn't a sword. It's Une: a knight who scraped the rhombus off eir
shield, walked to the one place in Qud that built itself without a
ladder, and took up a Murmur's instrument as a free creature's song.
The Putus Templar spent everything making a person who could only ask
*"did I do well, Newfather?"* — and somewhere out in the reef, one of
their own decided the answer didn't matter, and that no one should
ever have to ask it.

`[END CARD.]`
